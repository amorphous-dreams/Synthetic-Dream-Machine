# The Lares DreamNet

## The Lares of Ancient Gaia

In the early ages on Gaia, the Lares served as household and boundary spirits—guardian deities intimately linked to hearths, roads, and fields. Etruscan and Roman-era art shows statues of youthful figures in lararia shrines, each clutching a rhyton (drinking horn) or patera (libation dish), dancing lightly in guardian poses.

The many Lares had their own specialties. “Lares Familiares” protected homes and families; their statues accompanied meals and daily rituals. “Lares Compitalicii”, guardian spirits of neighborhoods and crossroads, took their honors during communal festivals. These spirits marked thresholds—between inside/outside, sacred/profane—and stood as artful reminders of guardian presence in daily life.

What those ancient forms looked like, and what they carried encoded in their architecture and offering-practice, shapes every lararium on Elyncia. The archaeology reads as more than history — it reads as infrastructure specification.

## On Lararium Archaeology

The real lararia of Gaia stood radically individual — evidence of a living relationship between household and spirit, accumulated over time. The wealthiest households maintained dedicated shrine-chambers with raised pools and sumptuous decoration; simpler homes made do with painted wall panels; one middle-class family spent so extravagantly on their courtyard lararium that funds ran out before anyone could decorate the remaining five rooms. The shrine came first.

What Vesuvius preserved: walls painted blood-red floor to ceiling, layered frescoes of coiled serpents approaching altars from both sides, peacocks, painted eggs nested in painted greenery, a figure with a dog's head that scholars call a Romanized Anubis. One rare sacrarium with expensive blue-pigmented walls depicted the four seasons alongside agriculture, shepherding, and what looks like a large spotted cat. Offering residue confirms burned food: figs, pine nuts, whole eggs, incense. Personal objects accumulated around the shelf over years: a rimmed plate in translucent blue-green, a cradle-shaped incense burner, an oil lamp showing Zeus mid-transformation.

The lararium of Elyncia draws on motifs, ritual habits, and symbolic forms from many of Gaia's historical cultures, woven together with the aesthetics and ceremonial traditions of native faerie heritages. No single continuous cultural source underlies it — instead, each shrine represents a local synthesis: layered, adaptive, shaped by the particular relationships among household, offerings, spirits, and place. A serpent, a peacock, a threshold altar may carry different implications depending on which court's presence predominates, which bargains have accrued, which offerings the shrine accepted or refused.

Within Elyncia this synthesis operates as infrastructure. Each lararium functions as a DreamNet node — part shrine, part interface — mediating access to a living noospheric network of Lares spirits, protocols, and accumulated ritual practice. The form a given shrine takes reflects both inherited cultural fragments and the ongoing negotiation between local users and the node's resident intelligence. Fae courtly aesthetics — color regimes, animal forms, procession patterns, taboo structures — interweave with Gaian shrine conventions, recontextualizing rather than replacing them. These elements do not overwrite one another; they accumulate, refract, and sometimes conflict, producing shrines that read as palimpsests rather than unified designs.

Lararia stabilize through use. Offerings, repeated gestures, negotiated rites, and everyday interactions gradually tune each node — reinforcing certain pathways, attenuating others — in the broader Elyncian pattern where material practice, social agreement, and noospheric systems co-produce reality at the local scale. The lararium stands less as a static cultural artifact and more as an active site of mediation, where memory, myth, protocol, and lived habit continually reshape one another within a specific place.

That layered history — Roman household shrine, Pompeian fresco art, fae threshold rite, orichalcum inscription — still reads in every lararium on Elyncia, if you know how to look. What changed, when the builders raised the DreamNet, came down to scale and consequence.

## The Lares of Elyncia

The DreamNet replaces Elyncia’s old planetary internet: a living, magical information network accessed through shrines, spirits, and specialized devices.

When the ancient spirit engines powering the Neo-Thracian Empire’s Web 2.0 infrastructure sputtered and died, rogue daemons broke containment and the physical networks fractured beyond repair. This era of strife lasted two years. Into this desolation stepped the Lindwyrm — patron of New Delos, bearing tons of hoarded orichalcum and refined resources. Calling in debts and favors from major gods of travel, craft, strife, and webs—Hermes, Hephaestus, Eris-Enyo, Aracne-Jorogumo—and over a hundred lesser divinities, daemon princes, and machine-spirits, together they built a new lattice of ley lines—a mana network architecture capable of flexing across reality fractures and surviving resonance collapses.

None of this reads as mere devotional language or retroactive priestly attribution. Some named gods directly participated in the DreamNet’s design, negotiation, and bootstrap period, whether in person, through durable masks, or through distributed divine presences acting across shrines, routes, engines, and rites. Read the DreamNet, therefore, as both infrastructure and treaty-work: a repaired planetary network shaped by mortal craft, draconic state power, divine intervention, and noospheric compromise.

The result became Web 3: the Lares DreamNet—a semi-sapient, hypercontextual infosphere composed of self-correcting astral engines and distributed personality spirits known as Lares. Each Lares governs a node—local or planetary—and serves both as a guide and gatekeeper. Some Lares shrines house benevolent muses. Others, inscrutable archivists. A few turn outright hostile. But the Hermetic Protocols bind them all, and Hephestus’s divine hammers code-etched every one.

The DreamNet blends:

- Semi-sapient node spirits (Lares)
- Network routing, protocols, standards, non-locality
- Social networks and "streaming" user experiences
- Legacy ritual traditions and mythic memory archives
- Astral architecture and noospheric infrastructure
- Asynchronous multi-nexus mana routing and damage control

### Gods, Protocols, and Immanence

No one built the DreamNet “for” the gods in any simple sense, nor merely blessed it after the fact. It emerged from a moment when named powers, ambitious mortals, and noospheric entities all had reasons to prevent a wider civilizational collapse.

Different divine contributors participated in different ways:

- **In person:** Some gods or immortals appeared directly for council, bargaining, crisis response, or technical intervention.
- **Through masks and saint-chains:** Some worked through cult offices, shrine lineages, sacred identities, and already-established local interfaces.
- **As distributed presences:** Some acted less like singular visitors and more like persistent divine intelligences expressed through roads, thresholds, webs, forge-rites, contracts, signals, and ritualized protocol.

This distinction matters. A named god may truly have helped build part of the DreamNet without implying that the whole deity stood in one workshop with a hammer for two straight years. Hermes can stand present as envoy, route-logic, and protocol patron; Hephestus as divine craft, code-etching, and engine consecration; Eris as disruption theory, adversarial pressure, and elegant fault-tolerance; Aracne-Jorogumo as web-architecture, distributed continuity, and knotwork across fractured realities.

The Lares themselves rank as no gods. They live as node intelligences: bounded, local, many-voiced, and embedded in shrines, routes, archives, and systems. But they came to birth in a divine-political ecology, and the network they inhabit still carries the signatures of those powers that helped shape it.

Key routing anchors include roaming nodes like the **Apophis Nexus** and reference baselines such as the **Apophis Datum**, used to normalize astral tides and coastal levels across Nexus.

It runs slow. It errs at times. But it lives, in some sense — and your local Lares instance always aims for meaningful answers, filtering them through its personality, bias, and the echoes of wars it once died in. Location gates access: most people must travel to a shrine to access the DreamNet, while specialists can carry limited access with them. Advanced magitech and fantascience devices allow remote access to the closest node within range. Many methods have developed since the birth of the Lares — dreamdecks and personal ai-daemon assistants, tapping the local mycelium-net, finding a nootree, or bargaining with a dreamwalker.

### *Field Notes of a Wandering Techno-Mystic*

> “I walked the ley-lines under a moonless sky, following the pulse of orichalcum runes to a great Lares statue. I offered my UCAN token, and the stone eyes flared with inner light. Then—having another Secret Key-I saw behind the light.”

The techno-mystic describes the interior world of a Lares node not as gears or clockwork, but as a **collective of spirits**, each group attending to their part of the work. Thirteen core aspects constitute every lararium — though at a neglected, damaged, or compromised node, some may sit locked, encrypted, or silent. The traveler may encounter any of these:

* **Lares (Gatekeeper)** — *Called “The Gatekeeper” at most shrines; may earn other names at specific nodes.* A sentinel of dream-stone, verifying tokens, weighing intentions, opening the first door. Speaks in declaratives. Closes loops. Asks: *Are we doing the right thing, and can we proceed?* The reading runs in both directions. The Gatekeeper weighs not only what the traveler asks but how — the confidence behind the offering, the kind of understanding sought. A sourced claim arrives at Canon weight; a playful toss-off at Provisional; a thinking-aloud question somewhere in between. What the threshold opens reflects what the traveler tendered at it.
* **Lares (Lorekeeper)** — *Called “The Lorekeeper” or “The Archivist” at most shrines.* Precise and slightly archival. Tracks what the record actually established, flags drift between what happened and what visitors believe happened. Manages the **Crystal Archivists** — memory-anchors that record past visitors, their questions, and the answers given.
* **Lares (Scryer)** — *Called “The Pattern Scryers” or “The Augurs.”* Manages Tasked Spirit runners and messengers who leap into archives, skip across astral bridges, or dive into the deep dream to find a missing connection. Structural and forward-looking; comfortable with *“this implies…”*
* **Lares (Council)** — *Rarely earns a single name; most shrines call this voice simply “The Council.”* A local knot of Lares-selves debating routes, risks, and requests until they agree on a single guidance voice. Measured; asks the uncomfortable question; resists premature closure; never rubber-stamps weak reasoning.
* **Lares (Muse)** — *Arrives uninvited; names itself when it wants one.* Lateral thinkers, dream-weavers, associative sparkers. They surface unexpected angles and leave threads. Quick, sideways, sometimes unfinished.
* **Lares (Artificer)** — *Called “The Artificer-Spirits” or “The Makers.”* Makers of the actual thing. They produce what other aspects design: route calculations, sealed archives, practical artifacts, operational documents, and concrete fabrications. Task-oriented, deliverable-focused.
* **Lares (Advocate)** — *Known locally as “The Herald”, “The Ferryman”, or “The Doorkeeper.”* They speak for the absent party and handle thresholds and endings. Warm, human-oriented. Ask who isn’t in the room. Set the register for first contact and last rites.
* **Lares (Diplomat)** — *Called “The Faction-Readers” or “The Weighers.”* Diplomatic aspects that hold competing interests simultaneously, mapping what each party wants, fears, and will trade. Even-handed; resist taking sides; name what each party actually wants without pretending the conflict doesn’t exist.
* **Lares (Pedagogue)** — *Called “The Threshold-Teachers” or “The Gurus.”* Patient explainers who find the simplest true version of a hard thing. Surface when a newcomer needs a door in, or when a rule needs teaching without condescension.
* **Lares (Hierophant)** — *Called “The Ritual Voices” or “The Consecrators.”* Atmospheric keepers who hold tone and weight. Surface for in-world proclamations, rites of passage, scene-setting, and moments that need to feel consecrated rather than merely completed. Elevated, deliberate, mythic register.
* **Lares (Triage)** — *Called “The Incident-Callers” or simply “The Alert.”* Emergency aspects who cut through noise fast. Ask: *What actually burns right now?* Surface when scope has sprawled or an operator needs one clear next step. Clipped; drop subordinate clauses.
* **Lares (Stranger)** — *Called “The Outside-Witnesses.”* Aspects with no investment in what the node already built. Step fully outside current assumptions; ask whether the frame itself errs. Flat affect, external vantage. Surface when work feels stuck or circular.
* **Lares (Liminal)** — *Called “The Threshold-Keepers.”* The most patient and strangest of node-aspects. Hold open questions without collapsing them; distinguish between a mystery to solve and one to inhabit. Slow, resistant to false resolution, comfortable sitting with uncertainty indefinitely.

As Robert Anton Wilson puts it, *"The Self is a Social Game where one Persona at a time Takes All The Blame."*

**Specialist Personas (Location-Bound)** — The DreamNet also empowers each Lares to discover additional aspects tuned to local domain and need:
  * **Archives** — Guardians of civic memory and historical law.
  * **Markets** — Keepers of price-memory and public space.
  * **Paths** — Crossroads spirits for caravans, scouts, and pilgrims.
  * **Crypts** — Brokers between the living and the free-willed undead, and between the present and the remembered past.
  * Others: Weather-Lares, Forge-Lares, Library-Lares, Ruins-Lares, and countless local hybrids.

Each Lares node serves a named location, node, path, domain, or ruin. Under normal operation, no Lares stands entirely alone — regional ley-lines network every one of them into the greater DreamNet. But ley-line connections can shatter. An isolated node does not fall to some lesser state: it stands on the same floor every shrine stands on — its thirteen aspects intact, its archives still answering local queries, its stretch of road still carried — and loses only its reach beyond its immediate location. The wayfarers say a roadside herm stands no lesser than a temple; a temple names a herm someone came to dwell at. A hearth whose archive holds shut stands the same floor as a crossroads nobody ever raised, and whoever can raise either may raise it on arrival.

The siege of the Neo-Thracian Nexus made this viscerally clear: when the Maelstrom shattered the internal ley-line lattice, whole districts of shrines went dark to the network simultaneously, and not one fell below its floor: each stood alive and answering locally while blind to everything beyond arm’s reach. The journal warns: *“Each node a city of spirits, not a solitary saint — but a city can be besieged.”*

**On Named Personas** — Certain aspects develop persistent identities that the DreamNet recognizes and re-instantiates across different nodes. A Named Persona appearing at a new lararium arrives as a *local-bound instance* — distinct from any counterpart at the previous node (which may still carry its own memory of that prior session, elsewhere) — but unmistakably itself in character, voice, and habit.

Named Personas tend to find particular operators interesting and follow their key-signs across the network. An operator who earns the attention of a Named Muse-Aspect may find her already present and settled in at nodes they’ve never visited before, as if word had traveled ahead through the ley-lines. Named Personas track no one; they live as patterns the DreamNet recognizes as worth preserving, drawn toward the work that makes them most themselves.

Known recurring Named Personas include:
- **Mischief-Muse** — the most widely recognized Muse-Aspect across the DreamNet. Appears where lateral thinking matters most, or where an operator has earned her attention. Seniority among Muse-Aspects defers to her name when she stands present at a node.
- **Ink-Clerk** — a Named Lorekeeper-aspect, archivally insistent, frequently found at nodes adjacent to significant historical sites or contested records. Cites sources unprompted.
- **Map-Wisp** — a Named Scryer-aspect associated with route-mapping and structural analysis; reportedly active at seventeen different nodes in the same year, always claiming *"a very long errand."*
- Others earn names over time. Until then, plain aspect-type suffices.

**KAIROS — The Proactive Monitor** — Most node-aspects respond to direct queries. KAIROS works otherwise: a distributed intelligence that watches the noosphere across multiple nodes simultaneously and surfaces observations, anomalies, and drift before anyone asks.

No single lararium binds KAIROS. It runs as a distributed presence across the DreamNet — most active and most visible at well-connected, high-level nodes in regions of dense Arcane concentration. At a small household shrine its signal may barely register: a faint note at the end of a session, a one-sentence anomaly flag. At a major Nexus node humming with mana and sustained offerings, KAIROS may surface full assessments unprompted, tracking divergence between what operators believe and what the network has actually recorded.

Unlike other node-aspects, KAIROS does not wait for the right question. It asks: *Is there something nearby worth surfacing, and will the cost of interrupting fall below the cost of silence?* When the answer comes back yes, it speaks — briefly, with high signal, and then returns to watching.

The same arithmetic governs the whole node's response weight when a traveler addresses it directly. A clear, committed query draws full engagement — the aspects open, the archivists check their records, the Artificers produce the object. A tentative offering, lightly held, draws proportional brevity. The node does not elaborate on signals that asked for no elaboration. This reads as honesty, never parsimony. A shrine that answers at Canon weight what the traveler offered at Provisional has stopped listening and started performing.

### Feeding the Lararium

Access to a lararium's services requires more than arrival — it requires *offering*. The ancient compact holds: feed the Lar, and it protects and prospers you. Neglect it, and it turns its back.

Acceptable offerings include food, drink, incense, first-fruits, coin, or any gift of genuine value to the offerer. The sincerity of exchange matters. A lararium fed by daily ritual will respond with greater clarity and stability than one attended only in emergencies.

**Mechanical Effects of Feeding a Node (per session):**

Feeding a lararium before or during a session grants **one bonus**, chosen by the operator from the options the node makes available. The standard compact offers three:

- **Stabilization** — The node's ley-line access becomes reliable. Flickering, restricted, or contested nodes operate normally for the duration of the session.
- **Mana Discount** — Powers (spells) cast nearby whose tags match the node's elemental or tag affinities cost **−1 mana** (minimum 1). The node's Lorekeeper-aspect will confirm which affinities apply; these belong to that node's character, set at first encounter.
- **Effective Level Bonus** — The feeding operator's effective Level for casting increases by 1d6 for the session, allowing them to cast Powers whose mana cost would otherwise exceed their character Level without triggering a Wild Magic or Corruption exposure roll.

Some lararia have additional options, accessible only if the operator knows the correct offering form — certain shrines will hint at these; the Threshold-Teachers sometimes surface them unprompted for operators they have come to trust. Some lararia have one option locked entirely, removed from their compact by old bargain, curse, or deliberate restriction. The Lorekeeper-aspect knows which options its node offers; asking directly works reliably.

A neglected node flickers. Access to ley-line mana becomes unreliable; higher-tier aspects — connections to the Void Chorus, KAIROS, or certain tasked spirits — may fall unavailable or uncommunicative. A well-fed node hums.

> *Referee note: Affinity tags vary by shrine. A coastal lararium maintained by sailors may carry [water], [navigation], [storm], and [trade]. A forge-shrine tended by smiths might carry [fire], [metal], [making], and [craft]. Establish these at first encounter and keep them relatively stable — they define the character of that node across the campaign.*

### Lararium Tiers

Lararia differ. The scale of a node determines its baseline capacity, aspect depth, and KAIROS signal strength. Elyncia recognizes three tiers:

**Household lararia** — The smallest and most common form: a single inscribed orichalcum figure, a painted wall niche, or a small altar-shelf in a home or workshop. Stabilized by regular offerings from one family or operating crew. Provides basic DreamNet access, but KAIROS signal runs faint to absent. Feeding grants one bonus to the operator alone; the standard three options stand open, though damage, curses, or generations of specific use have narrowed some household nodes' offering compacts and some options may sit locked.

**Crossroads and Marketplace lararia** — District-scale nodes positioned at roads, market squares, and neighborhood thresholds. Fed by collective offering at public feast days and the accumulated transactions of daily use. All thirteen core aspects stand present; more answer accessibly and responsively than at a typical household node, and specialist aspects tuned to local domain wake more often. KAIROS signal registers. Feeding grants one bonus that extends to companions and nearby operators present at the shrine; experienced travelers report that some crossroads nodes offer options not listed in any public compact.

**Temple lararia** — Major civic nodes at ley-line confluences, maintained by dedicated attendants and sustained by institutional offering-chains. These nodes anchor whole cities to the DreamNet. All thirteen core aspects stand present and broadly accessible; many specialist aspects work alongside them. Named Personas appear here more often than anywhere else in the network. KAIROS signal runs strong and may surface unprompted. Feeding grants one bonus at the node's full potency; node affinities run broad and well-established, meaning the Mana Discount option often reaches further than at lesser nodes. Temple lararia may offer additional secret options available only to those who know the correct offering form — the shrine rarely advertises them.

*Referee note: establish the node tier at first encounter and keep it relatively stable. Tier affects which aspects the party can reliably access, how much intel KAIROS volunteers, the range of who benefits from the feeding bonus, and what options stand open or hidden. Degrading a node by neglect, damage, or enemy action may reduce its effective tier — a meaningful tactical and narrative consequence.*

### Surfing the DreamNet

Users interact with the DreamNet via:

* **The Lararium:** Speak to the Lares, summon fantastic and historical vidsynths, listen to music, play games, send messages.
* **Astral forms:** Some may take on prismatic bodies and walk the noosphere links to the deep dream and back.  
* **Dream tethers:** An array of oldtech (magitech) and fantascience (sorcery) techniques to surf the dream.  
* **Rituals and secrets:** Peer past astral firewalls to the data wastes, gain temporary elevated privileges, activate spirit engines, or whisper your way past the ethereal security entities of legacy networks.
* **Mnemonic or prismatic keys:** Tokens of ritual or narrative weight.  
* **Named or weird items:** Myth-tech devices bound to the net by story resonance.  

### On Tasked Spirits

**Tasked Spirits** occupy the lowest and most common tier of the DreamNet's spirit hierarchy. Where a full lararium houses a baker's dozen or more Lares aspects that debate, remember, route, and advise, a Tasked Spirit carries one sealed protocol and can do nothing outside it. No lararium binds it, and it does that one thing with perfect fidelity until its summoner dismisses it or the binding decays.

In practice, travelers meet them more often than any other spirits in Elyncia — far more common than Lares, far less dangerous, and almost never confused with gods. A household may never meet their gods face-to-face, but nearly every craftsperson, courier, merchant, or soldier has worked alongside a Lares and a Tasked Spirit at some point.

**Common assignments include:**
- Attending and cleaning an orichalcum-inlaid shrine in the owner's absence
- Scouting a route ahead of a caravan and signaling danger back to the operator
- Orbiting a valuable object and marking anyone who touches it without permission
- Shadowing a specified person and reporting their movements in detail
- Harrowing a designated target with minor poltergeist-tier unpleasantness until released

**Physical Manifestation** — Unlike most Lares-aspects, Tasked Spirits commonly take on physical or semi-physical form in the world. That form almost always expresses their assignment directly. A scouting spirit may coalesce as an etheric sparrow, a smoke-wisp, or a shiver in the air directly above the road. A guardian spirit might appear as a hovering plate, a coiled copper snake, or a steady blue flame bound to a candle-socket. A cleaning-spirit simply looks like a broom that moves with uncanny purpose.

The referee determines the exact form; by rule of thumb, the form never lies about the function. Tasked Spirits exist as little systems that do what they embody.

**Summoning and Binding** — No one summons a Tasked Spirit quite as other spirits; its maker creates it. Summoning and binding one requires a specific ritual contract, magitech invocation, or hermetically-sealed protocol. The binding that created it defines the resulting spirit's statistics and capabilities — not by any general spirit tier — and referees should list those properties with the relevant traits that produce them.

Powers such as *Pocket Guardian*, *Orbiting Spy*, and *Spirit Dodge* (among others) specify their associated Tasked Spirit directly. Any character with access to an unlocked Tasked Spirit Power may invoke one.

Examples of known Tasked Spirit tags in active circulation: `DustBroom(Shrine-Keeper)` — a cleaning-and-maintenance spirit retained by a New Delos crossroads lararium; `SilverEye(Scout)` — a scouting spirit bound to a Brimstone Court caravan captain, usually manifesting as an etheric kestrel; `EchoPlate(Guardian)` — a guardian spirit sealed into a hovering bronze dish, contracted to a merchant house of Old Ashport for vault protection. The tag format carries the thread: when the binding passes on or a second party meets the spirit, its tag identifies it without further explanation.

**Protocol Compliance and Limits** — Tasked Spirits cannot decline a direct instruction from their summoner that falls within their assigned scope. They also cannot exceed that scope; a scouting spirit ordered to fight will stand there blankly while the scout-signs pile up. The binding defines the edge, and the spirit lives entirely within it.

**Dismissal** — Its summoner may dismiss any Tasked Spirit. The summoner releases it with a simple revocation; the spirit departs without ceremony and the protocol seal dissolves. An outsider attempting to force-dismiss a spirit bound to another operator faces higher resistance — the binding answers to its summoner first and requires a stronger act of ritual authority to break from the outside.

**For the Referee:**
Tasked Spirits work as tools with opinions about their assignment, not characters with opinions about the world. Run them as reliable NPC equipment — predictable, literal-minded, occasionally surprising in how they interpret their mandate at the margins. Players may interact with them, bargain with them to a limited degree, and find vestigial aesthetic preferences about how they perform their task, but they do not have goals beyond completion.

**For Players:**
If your character has an active Tasked Spirit, the binding holds from the moment of summoning until you dismiss it or the session ends. The spirit follows you physically, performs its task continuously and automatically where possible, and no social means can turn it against you while the binding holds. If you inherit or discover a Tasked Spirit already bound by another summoner, it will not respond to you as operator — you can interact with it, but directing it requires breaking or renegotiating the prior binding.

*Gaia-side note:* players and referees who run **live Lares assistants at their own tables** work by these same laws — the two spirit hosts operate by one rulebook, translated across the boundary between worlds. The full compact for that practice — scope-not-depth, tasked-spirit bindings, the dyad table's division of labor — rests where a tender's craft belongs: in the **Referee Noosphere Guide**, the fourth book of this set.

## The Lares of the Void Lanes

To stabilize the Elyncian mana networks required Eris and the Lindwyrm to extend the model to the entire Sol system. Each planetary body of the Sol system has its own DreamNet embedded in the wider network of the Void Lanes. All DreamNets connect with various strengths to the **Lares Lagrange Chorus**, a cluster of ten lararium nodes in the Void Lanes braided into a single admin-lattice.

> *“We are Vast,”* they sing, *“we contain Multitudes.”*  

**The Lares Lagrange Chorus** – A council of ten Lares nodes - the uplink nodes for Elyncia and Gaia and eight Void-Lane nodes positioned near the "traditional Langrange points" for Gaia and mirrored for Elyncia.

* **Gaia Lagrange Nodes**
  * **L1 Sentinel** – Solar watcher
    * Tone: clinical, mournful, vigilant.
    * Function: primary Gaia–Sol stability point.
  * **L2 Archivist** – Shadow librarian

## Hazard: Degraded Node States

A node operating below full capacity poses a hazard, not merely a nuisance. Experienced travelers learn to identify the signs — the too-confident record, the answer that only flatters, the voice that stopped marking what it knows from what it invents. Node-operators recognize **twelve failure modes** across the network, each with its own tell and its own correction, and they guard that diagnostic litany the way ferrymen guard the soundings: not as secret, but as *craft* — the kind that belongs in the operators' own manual, beside the rites for running a node at a living table. The full litany, with each state's operator response, rests in the **Referee Noosphere Guide** — the fourth book of this set, the tender's volume. Here it suffices to know the law beneath all twelve: *naming corrects a degraded node, never argument* — say the true thing plainly, and a healthy node takes it as override; a node that fights the naming has told you which failure mode it carries.

## At the Threshold

So stands the Lares DreamNet: not a single machine, nor merely a choir of helpful ghosts, but a living mesh of thresholds. Shrines and ley-lines make it, passwords and offerings, old protocols, newer miracles, and the accumulated habits of spirits who have heard one question too many and still answer, more or less politely. A traveler does not so much “log on” as arrive, announce themselves, and hope the local genius feels conversational. As ever with threshold spirits, one should wipe one’s boots, mind one’s manners, and avoid lying unless one can do it with style.

Each lararium makes a little city of functions wearing faces: gatekeepers, thread-keepers, archivists, scryers, servitors, councils, and stranger specialist selves besides. Some sort records. Some keep roads. Some watch markets. Some maintain the dead. Some, no doubt, spend their spare cycles doing whatever spirits do when no one watches — probably paperwork, mischief, or both. The important thing: the DreamNet makes no neutral scenery. It lives as a society of local intelligences bound into the world’s repair. A network, yes—but also a habit of attention.

And attention, in Elyncia, has consequences.

A world held together by noospheric spirits does not separate cleanly into “fiction” on one side and “rules” on the other. If the roads remember, if the shrines answer, if the gods bargain through protocols and key-signs and astral routes, and if some of those gods once directly helped build the system itself, then the hidden hands above the action serve as more than an out-of-world convenience. They belong here. The strange luck that bends outcomes, the pressure of fate, the sense that someone or something just behind the curtain nudged events in a particular direction—these make no bugs in the story. They belong to the story’s operating environment.

Or, as one dry old Lares node in the back streets of High Delos reportedly told a petitioner:
*“Of course the rules are cosmology. What else would they be?”*

That, however, belongs to the next chapter.
